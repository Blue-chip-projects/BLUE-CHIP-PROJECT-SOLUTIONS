FOUNDATIONS · PRODUCTION CONTROL

From master schedule to daily production

Learn why a construction master schedule needs a production-control layer to turn long-range intent into ready, executable daily work.

5 min read · browser narration

A schedule tells you what should happen. It does not automatically make the work executable.

A master programme is essential. It gives the project its logic, sequence, contractual dates and milestones. But the closer you get to the workface, the more questions appear that a long-range schedule cannot answer on its own.

Is the area available? Has the design information been released? Is the material on site? Is the predecessor genuinely complete? Is the right crew available? Has the responsible team actually committed to doing the work?

The execution gap

This is the gap between planned intent and production reality. A task may be scheduled to start on Monday, but Monday is only meaningful if the conditions required to start are already in place.

A useful rule: do not confuse “scheduled” with “ready”.

Use shorter control cycles

Production control brings the master schedule into a shorter horizon. Upcoming activities are reviewed early enough to remove blockers, organise the workfront and confirm the team that will execute them.

  1. Plan: understand what the programme says should happen.
  2. Make ready: test prerequisites and remove constraints.
  3. Commit: agree realistic work with the people responsible.
  4. Execute: record what actually starts and completes.
  5. Learn: understand variance and improve the next plan.

What changes on site?

The daily conversation becomes more precise. Instead of asking “Are we on programme?”, the team can ask “Which work is ready, who owns it, what has been promised today, and what is preventing the next activity from being released?”

That creates a traceable link from milestone to activity, from activity to workfront, from workfront to team, and from commitment to actual completion.

Try it on your project

Pick one milestone due in the next four to six weeks. Trace the activities feeding it. For each activity, list the conditions that must be true before the work can start. Then identify which of those conditions are not yet secured. You have just started turning a schedule into a production-control system.
